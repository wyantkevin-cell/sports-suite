What happens to your money over a season?

We played a season of 250 bets 10,000 times on a computer, starting with $10,000, and counted how the money ended up. “Middle result” is the result in the middle; “usually between” covers the middle 8 times out of 10. “Ahead” means finishing with more than the $10,000 you started with. “Worst dip” is the typical biggest fall from your highest point along the way. The “sized to the edge” way of betting grows the bet when the edge is bigger and shrinks it when the edge is smaller (a standard sizing rule called half-Kelly); it never puts more than 10% of what you have on one bet. With a real edge — same $100 every betWinning 55 times in 100 at −110 odds. Middle result: $11,155 (usually $9,245–$13,064). Ahead 81 times in 100. Typical worst dip 11%.
With a real edge — bet sized to the edgeSame 55-in-100 edge. Middle result: $12,609 (usually $7,453–$21,332). Ahead 73 times in 100. Typical worst dip 31% — higher in the middle, but wilder both ways.
No edge at all — same $100 every betA coin flip at −110 odds. Middle result: $8,864 (usually $6,955–$10,773). Ahead 23 times in 100. The book’s price quietly eats the money.
No edge at all — bet sized to the edgeWith no edge showing, this way of betting places no bets at all. You keep your $10,000. Not betting beats betting when the price is not in your favor.
Edge claimed, but not real — bet sized to the edgeBetting as if the 55-in-100 edge were real, when it is not. Middle result: $9,198 (usually $5,437–$15,561). Ahead 42 times in 100. How it works, in plain words.

This site helps you judge a sports bet before you place it. It gives a chance for each game and each player pick, scores a whole ticket from any book, runs a ticket 10,000 times to show how often it wins, and keeps a public record of how its picks did.

The numbers behind the picks are early-stage and still proving themselves, so the Our record tab shows every settled pick with its result and date — look there before you trust any number here.

Game forecasts (Who wins?)

Each game gets one headline number: the team we think wins, and how often we think it wins — for example, “Knicks 87%”. Under it, Model A (the other model) and Model B (our model) show the two numbers behind it, where their numbers are in this snapshot. A game close to 50-50 is marked as a close call — a lean, not a pick to lean on.

A chance is built from past games: how well teams score and stop the other team, recent form, rest, and playing at home, with recent games counting most. News that comes after the numbers are made — injuries and late lineup changes — is not in them, so check the news before you use a pick. Every number carries its date.

Player picks

Player picks give a chance that a player goes over or under a line — points, shots on goal, catches and yards, and more. The chance comes from recent games, with the most recent games counting most, and takes into account how strong the other team’s defense is against that kind of player. Warnings flag injuries and other reasons to be careful.

Touchdown picks work the same way, from how often a player gets the ball near the goal line, plus the weather and how the game is expected to play out. The chance shown is our guess, not a book’s price — hold it next to a real payout to see if the payout is worth it.

Picks in the same game can affect each other. When teammates are on the same ticket, one scoring can make another scoring less likely, so the real chance of the whole ticket is lower than simply combining the single chances. The ticket checker flags this when it happens.

The ticket checker (Check my ticket)

What “good value” means. Flip a coin with me. Heads, I pay you $2. Tails, you pay me $1. You’d take that all day, right? That’s a good-value bet — the price is wrong in your favor. Now flip it: heads you win $1, tails you lose $2. You’d walk away. Same coin, different price. A good-value bet pays you more than it should for how often it wins.

How it works. Upload a screenshot of a slip from any book. The checker lists the picks it found, and you can fix any of them by hand: player, kind of pick, line, over or under, and the chance. You can also add picks by hand or change a chance you disagree with — the score follows your number. Enter the payout exactly as shown on your slip. Your screenshot stays on your device; nothing is sent anywhere. FanDuel’s board is used as a market-wide reference for what a fair price looks like, but you can bet wherever you want.

The score. The score runs from 0 to 100. A score of 50 means a fair bet — the payout matches how often it wins. The score is 50 plus the value, so a ticket worth 20% more than a fair price scores 70, and one worth 15% less scores 35, stopping at 0 and 100. Alongside the score you get the value as a percentage, and the chance the whole ticket hits written as “1 in N” — for example, 1 in 7 means it should hit about once in every seven tries. A thumbs up starts at 60, meaning the payout is clearly better than fair, and a thumbs down means it is not. One plain sentence sums up the verdict. Each pick also shows our chance next to what the ticket pays like, so you can see which pick is carrying the ticket. For tickets where missing one pick still pays a little, the score counts that payout too. That is an average over many repeats of the same ticket — a single ticket either wins it all or loses it all.

The parlay simulator (Try a ticket 10,000 times)

The ticket checker scores one ticket you already have. The simulator is different: pick legs from today’s board — teams to win, plus player picks — and it runs that ticket 10,000 times before you place it once. You see the chance the whole ticket hits, how often you get 3 out of 4 right (the most common way to lose a 4-leg ticket), and what you would win or lose on average per $100 at the payout your book shows. It also shows how wild the ride is: one ticket is all or nothing, so the average only shows up over many tries. If you snap a ticket first, one tap runs that ticket 10,000 times instead of picking legs by hand. Legs we can’t match to today’s board are left out and named, so the chance shown is only for the legs that ran — never the whole ticket.

Game legs in the same game are run on the same simulated game, so their link is built in. Player picks in the same game as another leg are a different story — that link is not modelled here, so the simulator says so plainly and you should treat the chance as the best case. Spread and total legs are not offered on the site; the full simulator, which includes them, runs behind the scenes. Every chance comes from our models, from the board’s date — a leg with no model chance is never offered.

Your money over a season

The Our record tab also shows what happens to $10,000 over a season of 250 bets, played 10,000 times on a computer. Two ways of betting are shown: the same $100 on every bet, and a bet sized to the edge — bigger when the edge is bigger, smaller when it is smaller, never more than 10% of what you have (a standard sizing rule called half-Kelly). The numbers are a teaching example, not our record: one row shows what a real edge looks like (winning 55 times in 100 at −110 odds), one row shows the same bets with no edge at all, and one row shows what happens when you size bets for an edge you do not have. Our own picks will run through the same simulation once 30 of them have been graded — until then that part of the page says so plainly. No way of betting shown here is a promise that it wins: even with a real edge, the sized bets end higher in the middle but finish ahead less often and dip further along the way.

Our Models

These are the 30 models behind the numbers on this site — 29 Mordy models plus ALL_IN (Model A) as the 30th. Each one focuses on one job:

Model A (ALL_IN) — a second forecasting source built in-house, so the headline number is the average of two models.

  • Mordy Game (6 models: NFL, college football, NBA, MLB, NHL, college basketball) — picks the winner of each game.
  • Mordy TD — predicts whether an NFL player will score a touchdown.
  • Mordy Props (9 models) — predicts player stats: receiving yards, receptions, rushing yards, passing yards, NBA 3-pointers, and NHL shots on goal.
  • Mordy CBB Props (3 models) — predicts points, rebounds, and assists for college basketball players.
  • Mordy Totals (4 models: NFL, college football, college basketball, NBA) — predicts the total points scored in a game.
  • Mordy Spreads (3 models: NFL, college football, college basketball) — predicts the final score gap.
  • Mordy Soccer (6 leagues: Premier League, La Liga, Bundesliga, Serie A, Ligue 1, Eredivisie) — predicts win, draw, or loss for soccer matches.

Every model is tested against past games before it goes live, and we keep score in Our Record so you can see how they’ve done.
